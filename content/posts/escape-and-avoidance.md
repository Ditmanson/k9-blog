Negative reinforcement is how we bulletproof behaviors and work on stress, and we teach it through escape and avoidance. It comes after [food and play](/posts/food-and-play/), and it's sometimes called Traditional training.

{{< youtube SnMAGTHxG2Y >}}

That's four sessions of force fetch with [Griz](/posts/griz/). The point isn't the fetch. It's teaching a dog to handle stress, and to listen when there's no reward on the table.

## The two corners

Escape and avoidance covers the other two corners of operant conditioning: negative reinforcement and positive punishment. We tell the dog to do something, and enforce it with some sort of pressure.

{{< quadrant focus="trad" >}}

## Have a plan

Punishment is reactive training. It's never part of the plan. We use it for safety, clarity, or both. Negative reinforcement is a learning strategy, and it's proactive.

Say grandma comes over and your dog jumps on her and knocks her down.

| Reactive | Proactive |
| -- | -- |
| Positive punishment | Negative reinforcement |
| They jump, then we correct it | We teach it before she comes over |
| For safety and clarity | For learning |

Positive punishment might be used when they jump. The better strategy is to use escape and avoidance to teach your dog to sit and wait for grandma to greet them at their level.

## Why I use it

- **It teaches dogs to handle stress.** Stress is part of life. A dog who has practiced working through a little copes far better with the rest.
- **It bulletproofs behaviors** where food and play alone can't compete.
- **It interrupts a habit.** We stop what they're doing, show them a new path, and give them a say in how it turns out.
- **It's the stronger motivator.** For most dogs and most behaviors, working toward a goal doesn't motivate like escaping and avoiding a threat. That's true of most people, too.
- **It becomes rewarding.** [Here's why](#why-it-starts-to-feel-good).

## It's not foolproof

Food and play can't traumatize your dog. This can, if it's done wrong. Two rules keep it safe:

- **The pressure must always be escapable.** Pressure a dog can't escape leads to fear and learned helplessness.
- **Start low.** Begin below the level that changes behavior, and only go up until you see results.

I don't hit, alpha roll, yell, or use social pressure. They're unclear, too intense, and give the dog no way out. I consider all of them inhumane, yelling included.

But the world is full of aversives. Using them properly is usually the fastest way to fix the problem, and it can save your dog's life.

## Escape, then avoid

| Escape | Avoid |
| -- | -- |
| The pressure is on | The pressure never shows up |
| They do it to turn it off | They do it first |
| They learn there's a way out | They learn the command is enough |

## Day one

We start with leash pressure, usually on a [pinch collar](/posts/prong-collar/). It guides your dog forward, backward, into a sit, a down, or onto a place. The release is the reward.

It has the most uses, but it isn't the only pressure. Later we can layer on the [e-collar](/posts/e-collar/) for negative reinforcement, an invisible leash.

## Yes, it's stressful at first

New escape and avoidance training is highly stressful. You'll see a cortisol spike:

- **Ears back**
- **Tail stiff or tucked**
- **Plenty of resistance**

As the novelty wears off, so do the fear and anxiety. It's one reason we teach the exercise with food and play first.

## Markers for pressure

| I say | It means |
| -- | -- |
| *No* | A correction (positive punishment) |
| *Down* | Stay down and avoid the pressure |

*Down* is a command, and it's a continuation marker too. Your dog holds it even when something more exciting than you shows up.

Markers get stronger with use. That's why the pressure I use on my dogs is so light, and why a calm *no* works far better than a screamed "F\*$# STOP, FIDO!"

## How the pressure changes

{{< timeline >}}
1. **Escape.** Light pressure, and the behavior turns it off.
2. **Avoid.** They do the behavior before the pressure shows up.
3. **Proof it.** Through stress, and in highly stimulating places.
4. **Fade the leash.** The behavior holds without it.
{{< /timeline >}}

## 1. Obedience

- **Basic:** *sit*, *down*, *place*, and *here*, every time, not just when there's a reward on the table.
- **Advanced:** at a distance, for longer, through stress and distraction.

## 2. Recall

[Food and play](/posts/food-and-play/#2-recall) makes recall fast and fun. This is where we bulletproof it, somewhere stimulating enough to call for negative reinforcement or a correction.

The aversives I use are controlled and won't harm your dog. The ones the world uses to enforce a recall can be fatal, so we do this right.

## 3. Heeling

Leash pressure is how we fix pulling. It also turns a heel into something they hold around other dogs, traffic, and distractions. It's one way I manage Griz's fear of off-leash dogs.

{{< youtube ixEkOHD3-JQ >}}

## 4. Settling

The goal is to flatten your dog: lower the excitement, and teach them to settle when they're aroused. We add distance and duration to *place*, *down*, and *sit*, then fade the leash. It's how I teach my dogs to [relax](/posts/dogs-need-enrichment/#6-relax).

## Stress they don't get a say in

Anything your dog isn't comfortable with can be aversive. I think of it as two different things.

| They have a say | It's happening anyway |
| -- | -- |
| Leash pressure | Grooming |
| E-collar work | Being picked up |
| Force fetch | Muzzle training |
| | The vet's office |

On the left they can move with the pressure and escape or avoid it. On the right is stress management: this is going to happen, and they learn to deal with it.

## Why it starts to feel good

Avoiding something unpleasant runs through dopamine, the same system that responds to food and play.

- **[Oleson et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3087-12.2012):** in rats, dopamine rose at the warning when they went on to avoid a shock, and dipped when they only escaped it.
- **[Stelly et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1904249116):** dopamine worked like a safety signal, and its pattern matched which rats learned to avoid.
- **[Lopez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.04.006):** in mice, one area's dopamine was involved early in learning. Another's got stronger as they became experts.

These are rodents, not dogs, but I think the takeaway carries over. Once your dog knows how to turn the pressure off, getting it right feels good.

## Granny's rule

Escape and avoidance runs on two things: removing a threat, and access to safety. So we make safety great.

When safety comes with food or play, the brain switches faster from cortisol to dopamine. That's the Premack principle, aka [Granny's rule](/posts/food-and-play/#grannys-rule). Eat your vegetables, then you get dessert.
